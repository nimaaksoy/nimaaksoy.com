# Writing and editing for KDP — what earns reviews

The cover + metadata earn the click. The book itself earns the second sale, the review, and the read-through. This file is the craft layer.

---

## Fiction outline template

A 10-beat structure that works across most commercial fiction genres. Use as scaffolding, not as a cage.

| Beat | What it must do | Length |
|---|---|---|
| Opening hook | Establish genre, tone, central tension in the first 1–3 pages | First scene |
| Normal world under pressure | Show the protagonist's life with the tension already implicit | Chapter 1 |
| Inciting incident | A specific event forces the protagonist into a choice | End of act 1 |
| First turn | Protagonist commits to engaging with the main conflict | ~20% mark |
| Progressive complications | Raise stakes through action and consequences | Act 2A |
| Midpoint shift | New truth, new danger, or new goal — recontextualises the story | ~50% mark |
| Pressure phase | Strip away easy options; force the protagonist toward the hardest version of the conflict | Act 2B |
| Dark moment | Make failure feel genuinely possible. The protagonist hits bottom. | ~75% mark |
| Final confrontation | Deliver the core emotional and plot payoff | Climax |
| Resolution | Show the new normal; satisfy reader expectations | Final chapter |

**Common fiction failure modes:**

- Slow opening — first 10 pages are setup without a hook.
- Saggy middle — Act 2 has no midpoint shift, just more scenes.
- Weak dark moment — protagonist is never genuinely in danger of failing.
- Rushed resolution — climax happens but the emotional landing isn't earned.

## Nonfiction outline template

| Part | What it must do |
|---|---|
| **Promise** (Introduction) | State the specific result the reader will get. Name the audience. |
| **Problem** | Show why the reader is stuck right now. Validate the pain. |
| **Framework** | Give the central idea or method early — within the first 20% of the book. |
| **Chapters** | One chapter = one obstacle removed or one lever explained. |
| **Examples** | Use stories, case studies, worked examples. Avoid pure abstraction. |
| **Action steps** | End each chapter with concrete takeaways or exercises. |
| **Integration** | Show how the pieces work together as a system. |
| **Result** | Help the reader picture realistic transformation. |
| **Next step** | Point to implementation, workbook, sequel, or related resource. |

**Common nonfiction failure modes:**

- Promise too broad (*"transform your life"* — meaningless).
- Framework buried in chapter 7 instead of chapter 1.
- Chapters that overlap topically — reader feels lost on what's progressed.
- All theory, no examples.
- No action steps — book reads as a lecture, not a guide.

## The editing ladder

In order. Don't skip stages.

| Stage | What you're fixing | Who should do it | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Structural self-edit** | Promise, order, scope, missing pieces, dragging sections | You | Free (your time) |
| **Beta read** | Reader confusion, boredom, unmet expectations | 4–6 target readers | Free (gifted copies) |
| **Developmental edit** | Structure, pacing, clarity, argument, scene/chapter function | Professional editor | $0.03–$0.08 / word |
| **Line edit** | Sentence quality, transitions, repetition, rhythm | Professional editor | $0.02–$0.05 / word |
| **Copy edit** | Grammar, consistency, style, fact-check | Professional editor | $0.015–$0.03 / word |
| **Proofread** | Final typos in the formatted file | Different proofreader | $0.005–$0.015 / word |

**Critical rule**: never use the same eyes for two stages. The brain auto-fills what it expects to see. Different stages need different readers.

### Where to spend a tight budget

If you can't afford the full ladder, prioritise in this order:

1. **Copy edit + proofread** (cheapest professional stages, biggest visible quality jump)
2. **Cover design** (the click-driver)
3. **Beta reads** (free if you have any audience or community)
4. **Developmental edit** (most valuable but most expensive)

**Bad allocation**: spending $3000 on a developmental edit and zero on a cover. The cover earns the click; the edit refines the book. Skip the click, the edit doesn't matter.

## Self-edit checklist (structural pass)

Do this *before* sending to beta readers. Most issues you can spot yourself if you slow down.

### Fiction

- [ ] Opening hook lands in the first 3 pages. If not, cut backstory and start at the inciting incident.
- [ ] Every scene has a *goal* the protagonist is pursuing.
- [ ] Every chapter ends with a hook — question, threat, or decision pending.
- [ ] The midpoint shift recontextualises the story. If the second half could be skipped without losing meaning, the midpoint isn't strong enough.
- [ ] The dark moment makes failure feel possible. If the reader knows the protagonist will win easily, there's no tension.
- [ ] The climax delivers what the opening hook promised. Reader-expectation contract is honoured.
- [ ] No information dumps disguised as dialogue.
- [ ] No scenes that only exist to set up later scenes.

### Nonfiction

- [ ] Promise stated specifically in the intro. The reader knows what they'll get.
- [ ] Framework appears within the first 20% of the book.
- [ ] Each chapter has one core idea, not three.
- [ ] Every chapter has at least one concrete example or case study.
- [ ] Every chapter ends with action the reader can take.
- [ ] Chapters don't repeat each other's ground.
- [ ] The conclusion shows realistic transformation, not magical thinking.
- [ ] Next-step section points the reader somewhere concrete (workbook, sequel, related resource — not "go change your life").

## Voice and style

A few rules that hold across genres:

1. **Sentence length variation.** Long sentence, long sentence, long sentence is exhausting. Short. Long sentence, short sentence, medium sentence creates rhythm.
2. **Specific > general.** *"A red Honda Civic with a dented bumper"* beats *"an old car"*.
3. **Active > passive** in 80%+ of sentences. *"She closed the door"* beats *"The door was closed by her"*.
4. **Show, don't tell** at emotional beats. *"She gripped the edge of the table"* beats *"She was nervous"*.
5. **One idea per paragraph** in nonfiction. Reader needs to skim.
6. **Cut filler.** Words to look for in every pass: *very, really, just, that, somehow, sort of, kind of, in order to*.

Read your work aloud once before sending it to anyone. If you stumble on a sentence, the reader will too.

## What earns reviews

Reviews come from books that **deliver on the promise made by the cover and description**, and ideally exceed it. The single biggest review-killer is mismatch between promise and delivery.

If your description promises a "fast-paced thriller" and the book is a slow literary character study — even if the literary study is excellent — reviews will punish you. They came expecting fast-paced.

Match the book to the promise. Or rewrite the promise to match the book.
